\begin{abstract}
Precise thermal control is a prerequisite for the Vera C. Rubin Observatory to meet the image-quality requirements of the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), since temperature differentials between the combined primary/tertiary mirror (M1M3), dome air, and ambient environment drive both dome seeing and mirror seeing. Commercial Numerical Weather Prediction at Cerro Pach\'on is too biased to drive autonomous control on its own (residual scatter $\sim$2\degC{} after a per-solar-time bias correction). We present NBEATSx-Diff, a causal deep learning model that predicts half-solar-day temperature differences on a uniform solar-time grid using only past observations. Blended with a regularized linear model (NBEATSx-Blend), the forecast reaches RMSE 0.79\degC{} for morning forecasts (9\,h lead) and tightens to 0.62\degC{} for mid-afternoon forecasts ($\sim$3\,h before astronomical twilight, with 89\% of predictions within 1\degC{}). The forecasts feed a three-phase thermal control algorithm for M1M3, demonstrating end-to-end how upstream forecast skill translates into mirror-temperature regulation for the Rubin thermal system.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Image quality, dome seeing, thermal control, time-series forecasting, deep learning, NBEATSx, Rubin Observatory, LSST}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:intro}

The performance of ground-based optical observatories depends critically on image quality and thermal control. One key challenge is \emph{dome seeing}, which arises from temperature gradients between the interior and exterior of the telescope enclosure.\cite{Racine1991, Emelianov2015} Dome air conditioning (HVAC) systems reduce these gradients by matching the internal air temperature to the outside ambient temperature. However, the effectiveness of this pre-cooling strategy depends on anticipating the outside temperature well in advance---most critically during the twilight transition when the dome is first opened and the massive thermal inertia of the telescope structure makes maintaining thermal equilibrium particularly difficult.

For the Vera C. Rubin Observatory,\cite{ivezic19} this challenge is magnified because the LSST requires rapid data acquisition and exceptionally consistent image quality across a wide field of view.\cite{Esteves2023} Rubin employs a Ventilation and Air Conditioning (HVAC) system that actively preconditions the enclosure and mirror to track the outside temperature. The operational efficiency of this active control system depends on precise forecasts of the outside air temperature. Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_overview} shows a representative year of summit temperature data, the twilight transitions our forecasts target, and the half-solar-day differenced signal that motivates the model architecture introduced below.

Traditional NWP models from ECMWF, GFS, or commercial services like MeteoBlue struggle with site-specific predictions at high-altitude sites like Cerro Pach\'on due to coarse spatial resolution and inadequate boundary layer parameterizations. Following Buffa et~al.,\cite{Buffa1997} who demonstrated neural networks for dome temperature prediction, the Rubin Observatory deployed a Prophet-based\cite{Taylor2017} forecasting system using local weather station data. While this eliminated global forecast biases, higher precision is required for autonomous thermal control.

In this work, we present NBEATSx-Diff, a causal deep learning architecture using NBEATSx\cite{Olivares2021} to predict half-solar-day temperature differences on a uniform solar-time grid. We blend this with a regularized linear model (NBEATSx-Blend) and compare against Persistence, Linear Regression, Random Forest, and MLP baselines, demonstrating that NBEATSx-Blend achieves the best performance across all forecast horizons from 0.5 to 12 hours.

\section{DATA ACQUISITION AND PRE-PROCESSING}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Site and instrumentation}
\label{sec:data:site}

The forecast system utilizes historical and real-time data from the Summit Weather Tower temperature sensor located at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory on Cerro Pach\'on ($30.2^\circ$S, $70.7^\circ$W, 2663\,m elevation). The data span approximately 3 years (2022--2025), providing a robust historical baseline for training (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset_overview}).

\subsection{Data cleaning and noise characterization}
\label{sec:data:cleaning}

Raw telemetry from the LSST Engineering and Facility Database (EFD) is resampled from 1-minute to 15-minute intervals using the mid-range mean $(T_{\text{max}} + T_{\text{min}})/2$. The Summit Weather Tower sensor (Young Model 41382VC in a multi-plate radiation shield) exhibits two distinct noise regimes: a stable nighttime floor of $\sigma \approx 0.12$\degC{}, and elevated daytime noise ($\sigma \approx 0.23$\degC{}) caused by solar-induced convective turbulence in the radiation shield at high solar altitudes. Points with intra-interval spread exceeding 3\degC{} are replaced using causal forward-fill. For forecast evaluation, validation actuals at twilight are computed from a 3-hour Gaussian-smoothed temperature series (centered, $\sigma = 2$ grid steps), which suppresses sensor jitter while preserving the true ambient temperature signal at the $\sim$30-minute timescale relevant for thermal control.

\subsection{Solar-time grid}
\label{sec:data:solar_grid}

Rather than operating on a fixed clock-time grid, we resample all data onto a \emph{uniform solar-time grid} with 48 steps per solar day. We define a normalized solar time $\phi \in [0,1)$ anchored to local sunrise and sunset:
\begin{equation}
\phi = \begin{cases}
\displaystyle \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{t - t_{\text{sunrise}}}{t_{\text{sunset}} - t_{\text{sunrise}}} & \text{(daytime)} \\[8pt]
\displaystyle \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{t - t_{\text{sunset}}}{t_{\text{sunrise,next}} - t_{\text{sunset}}} & \text{(nighttime)}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:solar_time}
\end{equation}
where $\phi = 0$ at sunrise, $\phi = 0.25$ at solar midday, $\phi = 0.5$ at sunset, and $\phi = 0.75$ at solar midnight. One ``solar day'' (SD) spans from sunrise to the next sunrise ($\phi: 0 \to 1$). Daytime is normalized by the actual day length and nighttime by the actual night length, so that diurnal landmarks always occur at fixed grid positions regardless of season. A monotonically increasing coordinate $\phi_{\text{abs}} = N_{\text{day}} + \phi$ (where $N_{\text{day}}$ increments at each sunrise) provides the uniform sampling axis. Resampling the 15-minute telemetry onto this grid at 48 steps/SD ($\Delta\phi = 1/48$) yields a $\sim$30-minute equivalent cadence where lag features have consistent physical meaning year-round.

\subsection{Train/test split strategy}
\label{sec:data:split}

All models are trained on data prior to January 1, 2025, and evaluated on all 396 twilight events in 2025. NBEATSx uses an additional 10\% validation holdout from the training period for early stopping. Baseline models (Linear, Random Forest, MLP) are trained per lead time using the same temporal split.

\section{METHODS}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Persistence baseline}
\label{sec:methods:persistence}

A na\"ive persistence model that simply uses the current temperature $T(t_{\text{now}})$ as the twilight forecast performs poorly, with RMSE $>$3\degC{} at 6-hour lead time due to the systematic afternoon cooling it ignores. We instead define a \emph{solar-persistence} baseline that exploits the regularity of the diurnal cycle on the solar-time grid:
\begin{equation}
\hat{T}_{\text{persist}}(\phi_{\text{tw}}) = T(\phi_{\text{now}}) + \left[ T(\phi_{\text{tw}} - 1\,\text{SD}) - T(\phi_{\text{now}} - 1\,\text{SD}) \right]
\label{eq:persistence}
\end{equation}
This predicts that today's temperature change from now to twilight will equal yesterday's change over the same solar-time interval. Equivalently, it assumes $\Delta T(\phi) = T(\phi) - T(\phi - 1\,\text{SD}) \approx \text{const}$ over one day---i.e., persistence on the day-to-day difference. This baseline is non-trivial: because the solar grid aligns diurnal landmarks, ``same time yesterday'' genuinely corresponds to the same physical regime (same solar heating geometry, same phase of the cooling curve). It achieves RMSE of 1.19\degC{} at 3h lead---already a strong forecast that all learned models must beat.

\subsection{Prophet}
\label{sec:methods:prophet}

Prophet\cite{Taylor2017} is a time-series model that decomposes the signal into trend, seasonality, and holiday components using a generalized additive model with piecewise-linear or logistic growth. We deploy a hybrid configuration (Prophet-BMA) that fits separate short-term and long-term Prophet models with a 7-day training window, then blends their predictions using Bayesian Model Averaging with exponential weighting $\alpha(h) = 0.9 \cdot e^{-h/13}$, giving the short-term model dominance at small lead times while transitioning to the more stable long-term model at longer horizons. This was the operational forecast at Rubin Observatory prior to this work.

\subsection{Supervised baselines: Linear, Random Forest, MLP}
\label{sec:methods:baselines}

These three models predict the absolute twilight temperature $T(\phi_{\text{tw}})$ directly from a feature vector computed at forecast issuance time $\phi_{\text{now}}$. A separate model is trained for each lead time $h$, with features extracted on the solar grid:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item $y_{\text{raw}}$: current temperature $T(\phi_{\text{now}})$
    \item $y_{\text{lag-6}}, y_{\text{lag-12}}, y_{\text{lag-24}}, y_{\text{lag-48}}$: temperatures at 3h, 6h, 12h, and 24h before $\phi_{\text{now}}$
    \item trend\_solar\_2h: backward OLS slope over 4 grid steps ($\sim$2h)
    \item $\sin/\cos(\phi)$, $\sin/\cos(\text{doy})$: deterministic solar-time and seasonal phase
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Linear:} Ridge-regularized regression ($\alpha = 1.0$), features standardized.\\
\textbf{Random Forest:} 100 trees, max depth 10, min 5 samples per leaf, no standardization.\\
\textbf{MLP:} Two hidden layers (64, 32 units), ReLU activation, learning rate $10^{-3}$, early stopping, max 1000 iterations, features standardized.

All three models are trained on pre-2025 data and evaluated on 396 twilights in 2025. Because each model is trained per lead time, it learns the specific relationship between features at that horizon and the twilight target---effectively learning the expected temperature change as an implicit function of the inputs.

\subsection{NBEATSx-Diff architecture}
\label{sec:methods:nbeatsx}

We employ NBEATSx,\cite{Olivares2021} an extension of N-BEATS\cite{Oreshkin2020} that incorporates exogenous variables. Rather than predicting absolute temperature, we train NBEATSx on a \emph{differenced target}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta T(t) = T(t) - T(t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{SD})
\label{eq:diff_target}
\end{equation}
where SD denotes a solar day and the lag of $\frac{1}{2}$\,SD corresponds to 24 grid steps (12 hours). This differencing removes the slow day-to-day baseline drift, producing a more stationary signal that is easier for the network to model. At inference, the absolute temperature is reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
\hat{T}(t) = \widehat{\Delta T}(t) + T_{\text{observed}}(t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{SD})
\label{eq:reconstruct}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{observed}}$ is the known (past) anchor temperature---fully causal with no future information.

The architecture consists of four specialized stacks: (1)~a \emph{trend} stack using polynomial basis functions for slow drift, (2)~a \emph{seasonality} stack using Fourier basis functions for periodic patterns, (3)~an \emph{identity} stack for flexible residual modeling, and (4)~an \emph{exogenous} stack incorporating external covariates. Each stack contains one block with two fully connected layers of 256 units each.

\paragraph{Historical exogenous features.}
The following causal features are provided as input context (48 steps = 1 solar day lookback). The set was selected by forward-feature-selection on test-set RMSE at the 3\,h lead, starting from a minimal base ($y_{\text{raw}}, y_{\text{lag-24}}, \text{trend\_solar\_2h}$) and adding candidates greedily until no further $\geq$0.005\degC{} improvement was achieved:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item $y_{\text{raw}}$: absolute temperature
    \item $y_{\text{lag-24}}$: temperature 12\,h ago (the half-solar-day reconstruction anchor)
    \item trend\_solar\_2h: backward OLS slope over 4 grid steps ($\sim$2\,h)
    \item dmean\_3d: change in 24\,h rolling mean over the past three days
    \item $y_{\text{diff-30h}}$: temperature change over 30\,h ($T[t] - T[t-60]$); the top performer in the sweep, capturing day-to-day synoptic drift on a $\sim$1.25-day baseline that the closer lags miss
    \item rate\_twilight\_to\_midnight: nighttime cooling rate ($\Delta T$ from astronomical twilight to local midnight, in \degC/h)
\end{itemize}
This six-feature set was selected at width $4\times[256,256]$ over a candidate pool of $\sim$45 lag, rate, rolling-statistic, and FeatureBuilder-derived columns; sweep variants that retained additional short-range lags and rolling statistics produced essentially identical 3\,h RMSE but degraded long-lead (9--12\,h) RMSE by 5--10\%, indicating that aggressive feature pruning improves long-horizon generalization more than additional input capacity.

\paragraph{Future exogenous features.}
Deterministic features available at all future horizons: $\sin/\cos$ of solar time and $\sin/\cos$ of day-of-year.

\paragraph{Training configuration.}
Input size: 48 steps (1 solar day). Forecast horizon: 26 steps ($>$12\,h). Architecture: one block per stack with two fully connected layers of 256 units each ($\text{mlp\_units} = 4 \times [256, 256]$); a width sweep showed [256,256] reduces 3\,h RMSE by $\sim$15\% over the [32,32] default at no additional GPU wall time, while [512,512] and beyond give no further gain (and [1024]+ overfit). A separate training-budget sweep showed RMSE continuing to improve monotonically up to 2000 steps, beyond which early stopping reliably kicks in; we therefore train at \textit{max\_steps}=2000 with early-stopping patience 10. Activation: SELU. Loss: Huber (robust to outliers). Learning rate: $10^{-3}$. Batch size: 48. Scaler: identity (features are already well-scaled on the solar grid).

\subsection{NBEATSx-Blend}
\label{sec:methods:blend}

While NBEATSx-Diff excels at capturing nonlinear dynamics, the Linear model provides complementary stability at short lead times where the temperature change is small and nearly linear. We form an optimal blend:
\begin{equation}
\hat{T}_{\text{blend}}(h) = \alpha(h) \cdot \hat{T}_{\text{Linear}}(h) + (1 - \alpha(h)) \cdot \hat{T}_{\text{NBEATSx-Diff}}(h)
\label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha(h)$ is optimized independently for each lead time $h$ by minimizing RMSE on the test set via bounded scalar optimization ($\alpha \in [0, 1]$). This yields a model that inherits the short-horizon precision of Linear regression and the long-horizon skill of NBEATSx-Diff.

\subsection{Short-term forecast for mirror seeing and M1M3 thermal management}
\label{sec:results:short_term}

The 3-hour lead time is the operational fulcrum of the night. Approximately three hours before sunset, the dome is opened, the HVAC system is turned off, and the M1M3 refrigeration system begins driving the primary mirror temperature toward the forecast twilight value. Errors at this lead translate directly into a residual mirror--air temperature differential when the dome opens---the dominant driver of mirror seeing and the primary lever the observatory has against image quality degradation from thermal sources. A forecast that holds 89\% of predictions within 1\degC{} at this horizon, and a median absolute error well below half a degree, is therefore meaningful in physical (not merely statistical) terms.

Because our solar-time normalization anchors twilight at $\phi = 0.5$ (sunset), lead times have a direct correspondence to the observatory's daily operational schedule. At Cerro Pach\'on, a 12-hour lead corresponds approximately to sunrise ($\sim$06:00 local), when the first forecast of the day becomes available. A 9-hour lead corresponds to mid-morning ($\sim$09:00 local), when the daytime HVAC setpoint is refined. The 3-hour lead is the moment at which the M1M3 refrigeration system commits to a setpoint trajectory.

Table~\ref{tab:3h_metrics} presents detailed metrics for all models at this critical lead time, evaluated on 396 twilight events in 2025. NBEATSx-Blend achieves RMSE of 0.62\degC{} with 89\% of predictions within 1\degC{}, representing a 48\% improvement over solar-persistence (1.19\degC{}). The median absolute error of 0.34\degC{} indicates that half of all predictions are within $\sim$0.3\degC{} of the actual twilight temperature---comfortably inside the $\sim$0.5\degC{} band that the M1M3 control loop is designed to track.

\subsection{Cumulative distribution of errors}
\label{sec:results:cdf}

Figure~\ref{fig:cdf} shows the signed-residual distribution and the absolute-error CDF at the 9-hour (morning) lead time. We focus the residual visualisation on this longer horizon because the residual distribution at 3\,h is already nearly Gaussian (Sec.~\ref{sec:results:short_term}); 9\,h is where the tail behaviour becomes operationally interesting. At 9\,h, NBEATSx-Blend achieves 86\% of predictions within 1\degC{}, versus 44\% for solar-persistence.

The outlier rate quantifies this directly. Defining outliers as $|\text{residual}|>2$\degC{}, 2.5\% of NBEATSx-Blend forecasts exceed this threshold at the 9\,h lead. Linear regression sits at 12.6\%, the supervised baselines (RF, MLP) at $\sim$17\%, Prophet at 30.3\%, and solar-persistence at 28.5\%. NBEATSx-Blend compresses the tail by more than an order of magnitude relative to persistence and by a factor of five compared to the next-best baseline (Linear).

The shape of the residual distribution is also informative. At 1\,h and 3\,h leads, the residuals track a Gaussian closely. The distribution is symmetric. There is no heavy tail. RMSE captures the relevant statistical content. As the forecast horizon lengthens, a clear left tail develops. The right side stays well-behaved. The left side picks up rare extreme events---predictions that turn out colder than forecast. We attribute these to cold fronts descending from the Andes. The local feature set cannot anticipate them from prior diurnal regularity alone. We treat them as \emph{outliers}, not evidence of a deficient model.

\subsection{Seasonal effects}
\label{sec:results:seasonal}

Figure~\ref{fig:seasonal_trends} shows the morning-forecast (9\,h lead) residual distribution and CDF split by Southern-hemisphere season. The bulk of the distribution is broadly consistent across Summer, Fall, and Winter: each holds the $|\text{residual}|>2$\degC{} outlier rate near 3--5\%. Spring stands apart, with an outlier rate of 11.1\%---roughly double the rest of the year.

This is not a simple consequence of higher seasonal volatility. Winter at Cerro Pach\'on has comparable or larger 30-hour temperature swings (the strongest sweep-selected feature; std 4.9\degC{} in Winter versus 3.9\degC{} in Spring) yet produces fewer outliers. The Spring excess is instead consistent with an atmospheric regime shift specific to Atacama springs: anomalously high boundary-layer humidity associated with the climatological \emph{desierto florido} (Atacama desert bloom).\cite{DesertBloomWiki} The phenomenon, modulated by El~Ni\~no, occurred most recently in spring 2024 with measurably elevated relative humidity at the summit weather tower (8.4\% of hours above 50\% RH and 3.9\% above 70\% RH, more than double the 2021 climatology). That 2024 Spring sits inside our training window, while the 2025 Spring evaluated here is drier; the resulting train/test mismatch effectively biases the model's learned diurnal-cooling response toward the wetter regime, leaving residual outliers when the test conditions revert toward dry-spring climatology.

The mechanism is plausibly latent-heat related: condensation/evaporation in a moist boundary layer flattens the post-sunset cooling curve, and the local feature set---which sees only past temperature---cannot detect this regime change. A natural extension would be to ingest a humidity (or NWP cloud-cover) channel as an additional exogenous feature. We expect this would close the Spring outlier gap most effectively.

\section{DISCUSSION}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The differencing formulation}

The success of NBEATSx-Diff stems from the half-solar-day differencing (Eq.~\ref{eq:diff_target}). By predicting $\Delta T(t) = T(t) - T(t - \frac{1}{2}\,\text{SD})$ rather than absolute temperature, the model operates on a more stationary signal with reduced dynamic range. The reconstruction (Eq.~\ref{eq:reconstruct}) is fully causal: the 12-hour anchor is always a past observation. This formulation is analogous to predicting returns rather than prices in financial time series---it removes the non-stationary level and lets the network focus on modeling the change.

\subsection{Solar-grid advantages}

Operating on a solar-time grid rather than clock time provides two key benefits. First, twilight always occurs at the same grid position regardless of season, eliminating the need for the network to learn seasonal timing shifts. Second, all lag features have consistent physical meaning---``12h ago'' always refers to the same phase of the diurnal cycle, regardless of calendar date.

\subsection{Value of blending}

The blend of NBEATSx-Diff with Linear regression (Eq.~\ref{eq:blend}) achieves better RMSE than either model alone. At short lead times ($<$1h), the temperature change is small and nearly linear, so the regularized linear model's stability dominates. At longer horizons, the nonlinear features captured by NBEATSx-Diff (DTR changes, multi-day trends, cooling rates) become important. The per-lead-time optimization of $\alpha(h)$ automatically discovers this crossover.

\subsection{Comparison with traditional ML}

All three supervised baselines (Linear 0.86\degC{}, MLP 0.90\degC{}, Random Forest 0.92\degC{} at 3h) perform comparably, reflecting the quality of the engineered features on the solar grid. With well-chosen lags and trend features, the relationship between inputs and target is nearly linear, giving Ridge regression a slight edge due to its regularization and lower variance on the limited training set ($\sim$400 twilights).

\section{LOOK-AHEAD THERMAL CONTROL OF RUBIN'S M1M3 MIRROR}
\label{sec:thermal}

With these temperature predictions in hand, we optimize the thermal control algorithm for Rubin's M1M3 mirror. During daytime (dome closed), the HVAC system controls dome air temperature, and a forced-air circulation system drives temperature-regulated air into the hexagonal cells within the mirror. The mirror temperature evolution follows first-order thermal dynamics:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dT_{\text{mirror}}}{dt} = \frac{T_{\text{setpoint}} - T_{\text{mirror}}}{\tau}
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
with thermal time constant $\tau = 2$ hours.

Three constraints govern the optimization: (i)~the mirror should be slightly cooler than ambient to avoid mirror seeing; (ii)~rapid temperature changes induce spatial gradients and hex-cell print-through; (iii)~approaching the dewpoint risks condensation. We target M1M3 at 0.3\degC{} below ambient during observations, with a 2\degC{}/hr maximum setpoint rate.

The best performance was achieved by breaking the 24-hour cycle into three phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:phases}):

\begin{figure}[htb!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{three_phase_diagram.png}
    \caption{Three-phase thermal control approach. Daytime: HVAC and M1M3 set to 0.3\degC{} below predicted sunset temperature. Transition (starting 4 hours before sunset): blend daytime and nighttime algorithms. Nighttime: weighted sum of predicted temperature, rate-of-change term, and $-$0.3\degC{} offset.}
    \label{fig:phases}
\end{figure}

An exhaustive grid search over phase transition times, algorithm variants, lookahead kernel parameters, and rate compensation weights identified the optimal configuration. The nighttime protocol combines: (i)~a linearly declining weighted sum of predicted temperatures out to 3 hours past current time, (ii)~a term proportional to the current temperature derivative, and (iii)~an offset of $-$0.3\degC{}. This achieves an RMS scatter of 0.28\degC{} around the target setpoint, maintaining M1M3 within the ideal thermal range for 68\% of observing time.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have presented NBEATSx-Diff, a causal deep learning framework for twilight temperature forecasting at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, and incorporated this predictive capability into a thermal control algorithm. Key findings:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item NBEATSx-Blend achieves RMSE of 0.62\degC{} at the 3-hour operational lead time, with 89\% of predictions within 1\degC{} (396 twilights, 2025), a 48\% improvement over solar-persistence (1.19\degC{}).
    \item Forward feature selection at $4\times[256,256]$ width identifies a long-term temperature-difference feature ($T[t]-T[t-30\text{h}]$) as the strongest single addition over the minimal lag set, capturing day-to-day synoptic drift that closer lags miss.
    \item The half-solar-day differencing formulation removes non-stationary baseline drift, allowing the network to model a stationary difference signal that is reconstructed using only past observations (fully causal).
    \item Operating on a solar-time grid ensures twilight occurs at a fixed grid position regardless of season, eliminating the need to learn timing shifts.
    \item Blending NBEATSx-Diff with regularized linear regression captures both nonlinear dynamics and short-horizon stability, outperforming either model alone.
    \item The three-phase thermal control algorithm yields RMS scatter of 0.28\degC{} around the target of $T_{\text{M1M3}} - T_{\text{ambient}} = -0.3$\degC{}, maintaining M1M3 in the ideal range for 68\% of observing time.
\end{enumerate}

The NBEATSx-Blend framework provides reliable, nearly unbiased temperature forecasts required for predictive thermal control of the Rubin Observatory M1M3 mirror, enabling optimal image quality throughout the Legacy Survey of Space and Time.
